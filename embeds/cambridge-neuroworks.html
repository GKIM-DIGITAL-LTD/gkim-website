From Ian Morrison · GKIM Digital

What I heard

Cambridge NeuroWorks is not an accelerator in the conventional sense. It is a translation system: a nine-partner consortium that finds people and neurotechnology with real potential, and then assembles a bespoke combination of scientific, clinical, regulatory, product, investment and commercial capability around each one.

An opportunity applies a proposed use of an asset, led by a person or team, to address a need, through a chosen pathway, using an assembled support package — in pursuit of defined clinical and commercial outcomes.

Everything the organisation does seems to attach to that one sentence. The fellows change, the science changes, the routes to market change — but the sentence stays the same. That consistency is what makes the model powerful, and what makes it worth representing explicitly.

Why the model is genuinely hard to run

None of this is a criticism — it is inherent in what you have chosen to build. A distributed, human, judgement-led system carries structural challenges that grow with scale:

  • The knowledge lives in heads. Who to call, what worked last time, why an opportunity was routed one way and not another — much of it is carried by a small number of central people.
  • Every pathway is bespoke. That is the strength of the model, but it means consistency, comparability and institutional learning have to be fought for rather than falling out naturally.
  • Stalls are quiet. An opportunity rarely announces that it has stopped progressing. Slipping milestones, unresolved regulatory questions and missing clinical engagement accumulate silently.
  • Value arrives before the metrics do. Much of what Cambridge NeuroWorks creates — evidence gained, risk removed, options opened — happens long before revenue or valuation make it visible to funders.

The idea: a digital twin of the operating model

Not a CRM, and not software imposed on the organisation. The starting point would be to understand and codify how the system really works — its people, assets, needs, opportunities, resources, decisions and outcomes, and the relationships between them — and only then explore where AI can make that model more systematic, more visible and more scalable, without displacing the human judgement it depends on.

Three ways of looking at the same thing — choose a lens:

  1. 1
    Discover people, needs, science and ideas

    Researchers, clinicians, unmet clinical needs, underused IP, findings and technologies enter the system from across the consortium and beyond.

    The twin makes previously disconnected people and assets visible to each other — expanding the search space rather than just recording it.

    Output: a structured pool of people, needs, assets and ideas.

  2. 2
    Form opportunities connect person, asset, need and use

    An idea is not yet an opportunity. Value is created by connecting a capable team, a defensible asset, an important need and a plausible route to impact — and by exploring several uses of the same science rather than committing to the inventor's first proposition.

    AI can surface connections that would otherwise stay hidden: one lab's science answering another clinic's need; complementary teams worth combining.

    Output: explicit, testable opportunity hypotheses.

  3. 3
    Select and route assess potential, choose the pathway

    Not accept/reject, but routing: blue-sky exploration, proof of concept, licensing, further research, referral — or a considered stop.

    Consistent decision packs — evidence for and against, unresolved assumptions, comparative opportunities — so decisions rest on the same footing regardless of who is in the room.

    Output: a selected opportunity, a pathway, and a definition of success.

  4. 4
    Assemble match experts, facilities, partners and funding

    Your most distinctive gear: configuring a bespoke support system from a distributed ecosystem — clinicians, engineers, regulators, IP counsel, facilities, funders, mentors.

    Requirements matched against a living inventory of capability, with the reasoning shown — instead of relying mainly on who happens to know whom.

    Output: a resourced plan with named people, commitments and decision points.

  5. 5
    De-risk resolve the largest uncertainties first

    Convert critical uncertainty into evidence in the right order — scientific validity, clinical need, feasibility, IP, adoption — rather than advancing every workstream at once.

    An explicit model of what is known, assumed, uncertain and contradicted, with the next most valuable experiment or conversation recommended.

    Output: evidence, reduced risk, and an informed proceed / pivot / stop decision.

  6. 6
    Translate build clinical, product and regulatory readiness

    Parallel tracks — science, clinical, patient, product, regulatory, IP, market, economics — that turn together: a change of intended use ripples through study design, classification, funding and commercial model.

    The twin keeps those tracks coherent, flags the ripple effects, and preserves the reasoning behind every decision.

    Output: a clinically, technically and commercially coherent proposition.

  7. 7
    Mobilise venture, licence, partnership or programme

    Not every success should become a start-up. The system chooses among venture creation, licensing, industry partnership, NHS-led implementation, further funding — or stopping while preserving the learning.

    Route options compared on evidence, with investor, partner and funding matches drawn from the same living inventory.

    Output: a committed vehicle, team, capital source and plan.

  8. 8
    Scale impact adoption, investment and patient benefit

    Patient testing, approvals, adoption, revenue, follow-on investment, UK-retained technology — with Cambridge NeuroWorks' contribution remaining attributable even where others capture the commercial value.

    Output: clinical, commercial, scientific and ecosystem value.

  9. 9
    Compound every journey strengthens the next

    Playbooks, precedents, failure patterns, benchmarks, relationships and better selection criteria — retained by the organisation rather than dispersing when people move on.

    This is where the twin earns its keep long-term: Cambridge NeuroWorks builds a proprietary, improving understanding of how neurotechnology translation actually works.

    Output: a system that gets measurably better at cycle n+1.

  • Possibility value
  • Option value
  • Evidence value
  • Execution value
  • Outcome value
  • System value

Most of these forms of value are created before conventional financial metrics can see them. A twin that tracks evidence gained, risk removed and option value created is a more faithful measure of early-stage progress than revenue alone.

The nine stages sit on three organisation-wide rails. This is where the AI lives — observing, representing, recommending and learning — while scientific, clinical and investment decisions stay under accountable human control.

Knowledge & evidence

Every claim, assumption, experiment, decision and its provenance — a complete organisational memory that survives staff, adviser and fellow turnover.

Answers: what do we know, and why do we believe it?

Ecosystem & resources

A living map of experts, partners, facilities, funders, datasets and relationships across the consortium — matched to each opportunity's current requirements.

Answers: who and what could help this, now?

Governance & portfolio

Selection, stage gates, conflicts, resource allocation and attribution — giving leadership a live view of where the portfolio is progressing, stalling or concentrating.

Answers: where should our attention and capital go next?

A system like this only matters if it makes the humans in it more effective. In each case the pattern is the same: less time assembling information, more time on judgement, relationships and science.

The method behind the idea

It is fair to ask where the confidence comes from. Digitising business ideas is the specific thing I do — through a method and platform I have been developing called GEARS™, refined over fifteen years of building systems in healthcare, fintech and logistics.

GEARS treats a business as a governed system of named capabilities — gears. Each gear can be described in plain language, assigned to a human, an AI agent, a workflow or a service, and instrumented with its own knowledge, application and dashboard. Leadership sees the whole system through a cockpit that compares design intent with runtime reality — how the business is meant to work against how it is actually working.

Digitising an idea is never "build software for it". It follows one arc, and it starts with knowledge, not code:

  1. Discover the opportunity. Define the problem, the market, the stakeholders and the outcome the whole system exists to produce — for Cambridge NeuroWorks, something like "convert exceptional people and science into patient and economic value".
  2. Build the knowledge brain. Capture what the organisation actually knows — documents, decisions, relationships, the things your best people carry in their heads — into a knowledge base every gear can draw on.
  3. Design the capability system. Decompose the outcome into named gears — a hierarchy, a dependency graph, bounded partitions — and decide, gear by gear, what stays human, what becomes agentic, and what is mixed.
  4. Operate with AI assistance. Each gear gets its working surface: an app for the people running it, an assistant over its knowledge, and a dashboard showing its health.
  5. Evolve through evidence. Change is governed, not ad hoc: investigations tied to specific gears, hypotheses with confidence levels, bounded experiments, and a decision memory of what was tried and why.
Every gear carries the same five things:
  • a plain-language contract
  • an execution mode — human, agent, workflow or service
  • its own knowledge
  • its own app
  • its own dashboard

The grammar has proved repeatable across wholly different businesses — our own go-to-market engine, a biotech commercialisation strategy, and a live multi-entity telehealth operation. That repeatability is what gives me confidence it would hold for a translation system.

Read back through this lens, the nine stages above stop being a diagram. Each is a gear: describable, resourceable, assignable and governed. The opportunity is the object that flows through them. The three rails are the knowledge brain, the living inventory and the governance cockpit. And the "design intent versus runtime reality" comparison answers a question every distributed organisation struggles with — is the system actually working the way we believe it works?

The important consequence: Cambridge NeuroWorks would not be adopting someone else's software. It would be expressing its own distinctive operating model in a form that can be run, measured and continuously improved — with the human judgement kept exactly where you decide it belongs.

If it works, four things change

  1. You can see the whole system.

    People, opportunities, resources, decisions and value flows become visible in one place — including the quiet stalls that currently surface late.

  2. Better connections get made.

    The right science, people, needs and resources meet at the right time — by design, not only by fortunate introduction.

  3. Translation accelerates.

    The biggest uncertainties are resolved earliest, so evidence arrives sooner, capital works harder and weak ideas are retired kindly and fast.

  4. Intelligence compounds.

    Every completed, pivoted or stopped opportunity makes the next one more likely to succeed. The operating model itself becomes a strategic asset.
