Compliance assessment

The structure creates a direct and visible connection between laboratory funding, patient acquisition, and laboratory utilisation. Even under FMV compensation, regulators will ask why the laboratory is funding patient acquisition at all.

"The laboratory is paying to acquire patients who may receive laboratory testing."

This is the classic enforcement fact pattern examined under Anti-Kickback Statute, EKRA, and False Claims Act theories. The simplicity of the arrangement becomes a structural weakness — an auditor can draw a direct line between laboratory funding and patient generation with minimal inference.

Compliance assessment

Documentation improves materially, but the underlying arrangement remains substantially the same. Regulators routinely look through multiple agreements and evaluate the total economic relationship. Separating services into discrete contracts does not change the fundamental question regulators will ask.

"Why is the laboratory funding these activities?"

If the practical answer remains patient acquisition for laboratory testing opportunities, the compliance improvement is limited. Auditors frequently consolidate related agreements into a single economic arrangement, regardless of how they are structured on paper.

Compliance advantage — economic separation

This is the decisive distinction. Laboratories are not purchasing identifiable referrals. Instead, they participate in a physician-directed ecosystem where provider selection is independent, acquisition funding does not determine utilisation, and one laboratory's funding may ultimately benefit a competing laboratory.

The multi-laboratory dynamic is particularly important. If empirical data shows that funding provided by Laboratory A routinely results in utilisation of Laboratories B, C, and D, that evidence directly supports the position that laboratories are funding marketplace participation rather than purchasing referrals.

"Participating laboratories fund access to a physician-directed precision medicine marketplace operated by GKIM."

rather than:

"Laboratories pay marketers to obtain testing volume."

Remaining risk — answer prepared

Regulators may still ask why laboratories are willing to fund acquisition without a utilisation guarantee. The prepared answer: laboratories receive access to a managed precision medicine ecosystem, but receive no guarantee of referrals, testing volume, laboratory selection, or clinical utilisation. That answer must be supported by operational facts, not merely contractual language.

Conclusion

Recommendation

Model 3 is the preferred structure. It is the only arrangement that changes the underlying economic reality rather than repackaging the same relationship with improved documentation.

The critical design principle is that Model 3 intentionally disrupts the traditional linkage between funding and utilisation. Most problematic laboratory marketing structures involve money and testing volume flowing in the same direction. This architecture breaks that linkage by placing an independent clinical layer — the physician relationship — between acquisition funding and any testing decision.

Model 3 — decisive advantages

Why the structure holds under scrutiny

The following characteristics, taken together, constitute a materially different legal and commercial position from Models 1 and 2.

Independent physician decision-making — no pathway from funding to clinical action
Multiple participating laboratories — one lab's funding may benefit a competitor
Provider blindness to funding source — structurally enforced, not merely contractual
No guaranteed utilisation — laboratories fund access, not outcomes
GKIM ownership of CRM and infrastructure — arrangement is infrastructure-driven, not marketing-driven
Demonstrable separation between funding source and laboratory utilisation

Models 1 and 2 remain laboratory-funded marketing arrangements, regardless of how they are structured or documented. Model 3 is better understood as a physician-directed precision medicine marketplace supported by a centralised operational layer. Assuming the operational facts remain consistent with the structure described, it represents the strongest long-term architecture from a commercial, legal, compliance, and audit perspective.
